\noindent We formalize and bound $P(\tau_{\mathrm{ARF}} < \tau_{\mathrm{det}})$ without
any assumption on the dependence between the two clocks. Notation reminder: $\tau^*$
is the (known) drift instant ; $\tau_{\mathrm{ARF}}$ the delay until the first tree is
replaced, $\min_i \tau_i$ over $M$ trees ; $\tau_{\mathrm{det}}$ the delay until the
first external alarm, possibly $+\infty$ ; $F_A$ and $F_D$ the cumulative distribution
functions of $\tau_{\mathrm{ARF}}$ and $\tau_{\mathrm{det}}$ respectively ; $H$ the
post-drift observation horizon ($H = 2{,}000$) ; $\mathrm{Miss}$ the ``blind spot''
event, defined in Section~1.

\section{Defining the Miss Event}

Every later result in this section is a statement about $P_{\mathrm{miss}}$. None of them
means anything until $\mathrm{Miss}$ is an unambiguous event, and the natural reading ---
the forest repairs itself before the alarm sounds --- is ambiguous in exactly three
places: when the two clocks coincide, when the detector never fires, and when neither
mechanism ever acts. This section settles the three, and shows that a single definition
disposes of all of them.

\paragraph{1. Why a strict inequality.} Both clocks count integer steps, so nothing
prevents them from landing on the same one and $P(\tau_{\mathrm{ARF}} =
\tau_{\mathrm{det}})$ can be strictly positive: the choice is not cosmetic. No continuity
argument rules ties out either --- real-valued times can coincide as well, and these two
clocks are far from independent, being functionals of the same error stream. Whether ties
actually occur is thus an empirical matter, settled in Section~4; the convention itself
must be settled on physical grounds.

Read physically, an equality $\tau_{\mathrm{ARF}} = \tau_{\mathrm{det}} = t$ means the
alarm sounds \emph{exactly} when the forest repairs itself. The operator is informed at
that very instant and no information is lost --- unlike the blind spot proper, where the
alarm arrives too late or never at all. A tie is therefore a detection success, which
fixes the definition:
\[
  \mathrm{Miss} \;:=\; \{\tau_{\mathrm{ARF}} < \tau_{\mathrm{det}}\}
  \qquad\text{on the extended reals, with strict inequality.}
\]

Measured on the campaign, the convention carries almost no weight: among the runs where
both clocks are observed, ties occur $0$ times out of $1\,906$ at $\lambda = 8$, $6$ times
out of $778$ at $\lambda = 25$ ($0.77\%$ of them, $0.30\%$ of the campaign) and $0$ times
out of $1$ at $\lambda = 50$. Switching to the non-strict convention would move
$P_{\mathrm{miss}}$ by at most $0.003$, and only at $\lambda = 25$. The choice is thus
justified on principle and immaterial in fact --- Section~4 returns to \emph{why} ties are
this rare, which turns out to be part of the phenomenon rather than a technicality.

\paragraph{2. Why a silent repair counts as a miss.} On the event
$\{\tau_{\mathrm{ARF}} < +\infty,\ \tau_{\mathrm{det}} = +\infty\}$ the forest repaired
itself while the alarm never sounded. This is the blind spot in its purest form, and
strictly worse than $\tau_{\mathrm{ARF}} < \tau_{\mathrm{det}} < +\infty$, where the
operator is at least informed late: here the evidence is erased and nothing is ever
reported. A finite $\tau_{\mathrm{ARF}}$ is strictly smaller than $+\infty$, so the event
falls inside $\mathrm{Miss}$ with no special handling.

That it should is not merely a convention. Excluding it would restrict the count to runs
where the detector eventually spoke --- precisely the selection bias this question holds
against the manuscript, since the runs discarded are those where the detector failed most
completely. At $\lambda = 50$, where $1\,999$ of the $2\,000$ runs never fire, such an
exclusion would compute a rate over a single run and erase the blind spot from the
statistic exactly where it is total.

\paragraph{3. Why double starvation does not.} On the event
$\{\tau_{\mathrm{ARF}} = +\infty,\ \tau_{\mathrm{det}} = +\infty\}$ neither did the forest
ever repair itself, nor did the alarm ever sound. This is qualitatively different from the
paradox under study, which rests on a \emph{silent repair} erasing the evidence before the
alarm gets its chance. If the repair never happened, there is nothing to erase: the model
simply stays broken, and no mechanism has cheated. Since $\infty < \infty$ is false, the
event is automatically excluded --- again with no special handling.

\begin{center}
\fbox{\parbox{0.93\textwidth}{\textbf{Adopted convention.}
\[ \mathrm{Miss} := \{\tau_{\mathrm{ARF}} < \tau_{\mathrm{det}}\} \qquad \text{(strict inequality, extended reals)} \]
One definition settles the three cases: a tie is classified on the detector's side, a
silent repair is a miss, a double starvation is not. No case needs a rule of its own ---
which is what makes the definition trustworthy rather than fitted to the answer. The
material weight of the tie convention is measured in Section~4.}}
\end{center}

\section{Making the Miss Probability Observable}

$\mathrm{Miss} = \{\tau_{\mathrm{ARF}} < \tau_{\mathrm{det}}\}$ is defined on the complete
race, with no time limit. The campaign observes $H = 2\,000$ steps. On runs where neither
clock has fired by then, the winner is simply not known, and $P_{\mathrm{miss}}$ depends
on what was never seen: it is not, as it stands, computable from the data.

Two ways out are available and both are unacceptable. Assuming that censored runs behave
like the others cannot be checked. Discarding them reintroduces the selection bias of
Section~1, and they are exactly the runs the phenomenon is about. The aim here is
therefore to replace $P_{\mathrm{miss}}$ by the probability of an event that \emph{is}
observable --- or, failing an exact rewriting, to trap it between two such probabilities.

\paragraph{1. What the horizon lets us see.} Absolute censoring is
$C_H := \{\min(\tau_{\mathrm{ARF}},\tau_{\mathrm{det}}) > H\}$: neither event has happened
by the horizon, so the race is undecided. Conversely $\{\tau_{\mathrm{ARF}} \le H\}$ and
$\{\tau_{\mathrm{det}} \le H\}$ are observable, and sorting runs by these two gives a
partition in which the verdict is known everywhere except one cell.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{cccl}
\toprule
Region & $\tau_{\mathrm{ARF}}$ observed? & $\tau_{\mathrm{det}}$ observed? & Verdict \\
\midrule
1 & $\le H$ & $\le H$ & Miss decided directly (direct comparison) \\
2 & $\le H$ & $> H$ & Certain Miss --- $\tau_{\mathrm{ARF}} \le H < \tau_{\mathrm{det}}$ \\
3 & $> H$ & $\le H$ & Never a Miss --- $\tau_{\mathrm{det}} \le H < \tau_{\mathrm{ARF}}$ \\
4 & $> H$ & $> H$ & Unknown --- this is $C_H$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Regions 1 and 2 together are exactly $\{\tau_{\mathrm{ARF}} \le H\} \cap \mathrm{Miss}$, a
probability computable from the data with no assumption about what happens beyond $H$.
Region 3 is never a Miss. Only region 4 is undetermined.

\paragraph{2. Trapping the unknown between two computable numbers.} Since region 4 is the
only undetermined one, it can shift the total by its own mass and no more. Counting it as
never a Miss gives the lower bound, counting it entirely as Miss gives the upper one:
\[
  \underbrace{P(\{\tau_{\mathrm{ARF}}\le H\}\cap\mathrm{Miss})}_{\text{computable, no assumption}}
  \;\le\; P_{\mathrm{miss}} \;\le\;
  P(\{\tau_{\mathrm{ARF}}\le H\}\cap\mathrm{Miss}) + P(C_H)
\]
This is a Manski-type bound: partial identification. Nothing is assumed about the
unobserved runs --- their possible influence is simply bounded by how many there are. The
width of the interval is exactly $P(C_H)$, which is to say exactly the extent of our
ignorance.

\paragraph{3. On this campaign, the interval collapses.} Section~4 measures
$P(C_H) = 0$: the forest, running at maximal reactivity ($c_{\mathrm{int}} = 1$), replaces
a tree before the horizon on every one of the $2\,000$ runs --- the largest
$\tau_{\mathrm{ARF}}$ observed is $1\,293$ steps. The minimum of the two clocks is
therefore always finite, the race always decided, and the two bounds coincide:
\[
  P_{\mathrm{miss}} \;=\; P(\{\tau_{\mathrm{ARF}} \le H\} \cap \mathrm{Miss}).
\]

\begin{center}
\fbox{\parbox{0.93\textwidth}{\textbf{Result.} The unconditional miss probability equals
the probability of an event observable within the horizon. This is an equality, not an
assumption: the naive computation would have returned the same number, but nothing would
have told us it was right. What the construction adds is the guarantee --- and the
condition under which it would fail. Were the forest able to stay inert past $H$,
$P_{\mathrm{miss}}$ would cease to be identified and only the interval above would
survive.}}
\end{center}

\section{Universal Fréchet Bound}

We return to the case with no finite horizon: from the marginal laws $F_A$ and $F_D$
alone, without any dependence assumption, we bound $P_{\mathrm{miss}}$.

\paragraph{Lower bound.} For a fixed integer $s$, the event
$\{\tau_{\mathrm{ARF}}\le s < \tau_{\mathrm{det}}\}$ always implies
$\tau_{\mathrm{ARF}}<\tau_{\mathrm{det}}$ --- it is therefore a subset of
$\mathrm{Miss}$, for any $s$. Write $A = \{\tau_{\mathrm{ARF}} \le s\}$ and
$B = \{\tau_{\mathrm{det}} > s\}$, so that $P(A) = F_A(s)$ and $P(B) = 1 - F_D(s)$.
Fréchet's inequality for an intersection follows from $P(A \cup B) \le 1$:
\[
  P(A \cap B) \;=\; P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cup B) \;\ge\; P(A) + P(B) - 1 ,
\]
and since a probability is non-negative, $P(A \cap B) \ge \max\big(0, P(A)+P(B)-1\big)$.
Substituting the two marginals, $P(A) + P(B) - 1 = F_A(s) + \bigl(1 - F_D(s)\bigr) - 1
= F_A(s) - F_D(s)$, hence
\[
  P(\tau_{\mathrm{ARF}}\le s,\ \tau_{\mathrm{det}}>s) \;\ge\; \max\big(0,\ F_A(s) - F_D(s)\big),
\]
an inequality that uses the two marginal laws only, and assumes nothing about how the two
clocks are related.
and since the inclusion holds for every $s$, we optimize by taking the largest value:
\[
  P_{\mathrm{miss}} \;\ge\; \max\Big(0,\ \sup_{s\in\mathbb{Z}} \big[F_A(s)-F_D(s)\big]\Big).
\]

\paragraph{Upper bound.} We partition on $\{\tau_{\mathrm{ARF}}\le s\}$ and
$\{\tau_{\mathrm{ARF}}\ge s+1\}$. If $\tau_{\mathrm{ARF}}\le s$, the inclusion in
$\mathrm{Miss}$ is trivial. If $\tau_{\mathrm{ARF}}\ge s+1$ and $\mathrm{Miss}$ holds,
then $\tau_{\mathrm{det}}>\tau_{\mathrm{ARF}}\ge s+1$, hence $\tau_{\mathrm{det}}>s+1$.
In both cases:
\[
  \mathrm{Miss} \;\subseteq\; \{\tau_{\mathrm{ARF}}\le s\} \cup \{\tau_{\mathrm{det}}> s+1\}.
\]
Boole's subadditivity, followed by optimizing over $s$ (and the trivial truncation at
1, since a probability never exceeds 1), gives
\[
  P_{\mathrm{miss}} \;\le\; \min\Big(1,\ \inf_{s\in\mathbb{Z}} \big[F_A(s) + 1 - F_D(s+1)\big]\Big).
\]

\begin{center}
\fbox{\parbox{0.93\textwidth}{\textbf{Result --- final bound.}
\[
  \max\Big(0,\sup_s[F_A(s)-F_D(s)]\Big) \;\le\; P_{\mathrm{miss}} \;\le\;
  \min\Big(1,\inf_s[F_A(s)+1-F_D(s+1)]\Big)
\]
Valid for any dependence structure between $\tau_{\mathrm{ARF}}$ and
$\tau_{\mathrm{det}}$ --- which is what makes it a \emph{universal} bound.}}
\end{center}

\paragraph{Verification on a toy case.} Support $\{1,2\}$, uniform marginals:
$F_A(1)=F_D(1)=\tfrac12$, $F_A(2)=F_D(2)=1$.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{llcl}
\toprule
Coupling & Description & $P(\mathrm{Miss})$ & Position \\
\midrule
Comonotone & $\tau_{\mathrm{ARF}} = \tau_{\mathrm{det}}$ always & $0$ & = lower bound \\
Independent & independent draws & $0.25$ & strictly interior \\
Anti-monotone & one equals 1 when the other equals 2 & $0.5$ & = upper bound \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Quick check: $\sup_s[F_A(s)-F_D(s)]=0$ (identical marginals) gives a lower bound of
$0$, attained by the comonotone coupling. And
$\inf_s[F_A(s)+1-F_D(s+1)]=0.5$ (attained at $s=0$ and $s=1$) gives an upper bound of
$0.5$, attained by the anti-monotone coupling. The formula holds, and both bounds are
attained exactly: the bound is the tightest possible on this toy case.

\paragraph{The bound on the estimated marginals.} Evaluating the two formulas on
$\hat F_A$ and $\hat F_D$, amplitude by amplitude (the $100$ seeds of a cell are i.i.d.,
which pooling across amplitudes would not be), gives the following. $\hat F_D$ is
sub-stochastic: a censored $\tau_{\mathrm{det}}$ never enters it, so its plateau sits at
one minus the cell's censoring rate.

\noindent Three readings. First, the bound holds on \emph{all} $60$ cells of the grid
($20$ amplitudes $\times$ three thresholds): $P_{\mathrm{miss}}$ lands inside
$20/20$ times at each $\lambda$. This is the verification of Section~3 on real
marginals, the toy case of the previous paragraph having only established that the
formula is tight in the best case.

Second, the bound is not vacuous, which is worth stating because universal bounds
usually are. The mean width per amplitude is $0.195$ at $\lambda = 8$ and $0.106$ at
$\lambda = 25$; at $\lambda = 50$ it is $0.001$, i.e.\ the bound is numerically exact.
The reason is instructive: at $\lambda = 50$ the detector almost never alarms, $\hat F_D$
is flat at $0$, and both formulas degenerate to $P_{\mathrm{miss}} = 1$ on their own.
The universal bound is sharpest exactly where the blind spot is total.

Third, and most usefully, at $\Delta e = 0.028$ and $\lambda = 8$ the \emph{lower} bound
is $0.91$. One can therefore prove $P_{\mathrm{miss}} \ge 91\%$ at the weakest amplitude
\emph{without any assumption whatsoever} about how the two clocks depend on each other
--- from the marginal laws alone. At the bottom of the grid, the blind spot is not an
artefact of an independence hypothesis: it is a consequence of the marginals.

\paragraph{Why amplitude by amplitude.} Applying the same two formulas to the $2{,}000$
runs pooled gives $[0.048;0.485]$ at $\lambda = 8$ --- a width of $0.437$, against
$0.195$ on average per amplitude. Pooling mixes cells whose difficulty differs by two
orders of magnitude, and the marginals of a mixture are compatible with far more joint
behaviours than the marginals of any one cell. The loss is not an artefact of the bound
but a real loss of information, and it is the same caution that Q~D.1 attaches to its
grid totals.

\section{The Race, Measured}
\label{sec:measured}

The apparatus above is now read on the instrumented campaign of Q~C/D: $20$ amplitudes
$\times\ 100$ seeds $=2{,}000$ runs, $H=2{,}000$. Everything in this section comes from
\texttt{QCD\_indicateurs\_full} and is reproduced by
\texttt{resultats\_R2/scripts/chiffres\_QA.py}.

\paragraph{One measurement governs the rest.} $\tau_{\mathrm{ARF}}$ is \emph{never}
censored: $0$ runs out of $2{,}000$, the largest value observed being $1{,}293$ steps,
well inside the horizon. The forest always repairs itself before $H$. Three consequences
follow immediately, and they are the answers to the questions Sections 1 and 2 left open.

\paragraph{Double starvation never occurs.} The event
$\{\tau_{\mathrm{ARF}}=+\infty,\ \tau_{\mathrm{det}}=+\infty\}$ has empirical frequency
$0$ at all three thresholds. Its treatment in Section~1 remains necessary --- nothing
in the model forbids it, and a campaign with a shorter horizon or a slower forest would
produce it --- but on this grid the convention is never actually exercised.

\paragraph{The identification problem does not bind here.} Since
$\{\tau_{\mathrm{ARF}}>H\}$ is empty, regions 3 and 4 of the partition are both empty,
and in particular $C_H=\varnothing$. The width of the Manski bound, which Section~2
shows to be exactly $P(C_H)$, is therefore $0$: on this campaign $P_{\mathrm{miss}}$ is
not partially but \emph{point} identified. The bound is not vacuous, it is simply not
binding --- a fact about this experimental design, not about the method.

\noindent Region 1 is decided by direct comparison, region 2 is a certain Miss
($\tau_{\mathrm{ARF}}\le H<\tau_{\mathrm{det}}$), and regions 3 and 4 are empty. The
$\lambda=8$ value cross-checks against Q~D.1, which counts the complementary event and
reports $\tau_{\mathrm{det}}<\tau_{\mathrm{ARF}}$ in $1{,}819$ of $2{,}000$ runs: the
two counts sum to $2{,}000$ exactly, with no run left over --- which is also how one
sees that ties are absent at this threshold.

\paragraph{Ties are rare, but not for the reason one might assume.} Over the runs where
both clocks are observed, $\tau_{\mathrm{ARF}}=\tau_{\mathrm{det}}$ occurs $0$ times at
$\lambda=8$, $6$ times out of $778$ at $\lambda=25$ ($0.77\%$ of them, $0.30\%$ of the
campaign), and $0$ times at $\lambda=50$. Switching to the non-strict convention would
move $P_{\mathrm{miss}}$ by at most $0.003$, and only at $\lambda=25$. So the choice made
in Section~1 is immaterial \emph{in fact} --- but not because of any underlying
continuity: both clocks are integer step counts and could easily coincide. They rarely
do because the two mechanisms operate on visibly different time scales, which is the
substance of the paradox rather than a technicality.

\paragraph{The aggregate is not the phenomenon.} These three totals pool cells of very
unequal difficulty and should not be quoted alone. At $\lambda=8$, $P_{\mathrm{miss}}$
runs from $0.01$ at the top of the grid to $1.00$ at $\Delta e=0.028$, where the
detector never alarms at all within the horizon: the aggregate $9.05\%$ is carried
almost entirely by that single weakest amplitude. Table~2 and Figure~2 of Q~D.1 give the
per-cell breakdown, with a Wilson interval and a censoring rate for each cell.

\section{Outlook}

The three bounds are established and evaluated, and $P_{\mathrm{miss}}$ is point
identified on this campaign (Section~\ref{sec:measured}), so what the Fréchet interval
now measures is what the \emph{marginals alone} concede --- between $0.09$ and $0.34$ of
probability mass at $\lambda = 8$, depending on the amplitude. Closing that residual gap
is not a matter of more data: it requires knowing the \emph{structure} of the dependence,
which is precisely the object of Question~B, where independence conditional on the stream
yields a sharper law than any knowledge of the marginals can. The natural continuation is
therefore to ask how much of this gap the conditional factorization of Q~B.1 actually
recovers --- not attempted here.
