\noindent This section justifies the association coefficient used throughout Q~D. The
counter-example of Section~\ref{sec:contre} comes from
\texttt{scripts/contre\_exemple\_kendall.py}, which computes both coefficients twice, once
from their definition and once through \texttt{scipy}, so that each controls the other.
Census figures come from the campaign of 2\,000 runs (20 amplitudes $\times$ 100 seeds,
$H = 2\,000$, $M = 10$ trees in the adaptive random forest, ARF); the bootstrap confidence
intervals (CI) of Section~\ref{sec:features} are written by
\texttt{scripts/derives\_QD.py}.

\section{Three Features of the Data}
\label{sec:features}

The quantities to be correlated are durations measured on a finite horizon $H = 2\,000$.
Three features bear on the choice of coefficient.

\paragraph{Non-monotonicity.} The median $\tau_{\mathrm{ARF}}$ is $118$ steps at
$\Delta e = 0.028$, rises to $346$ at $\Delta e = 0.085$, then falls steadily to $28$ at
the top of the grid: a range of $28$ to $346$, a factor of $12.4$, and not monotone in
$\Delta e$. The rise is not an artefact of the median. The means move the same way
($162.7$ then $385.9$), and the bootstrap confidence intervals of the two medians are
disjoint, $[92.5 ; 158.5]$ against $[281 ; 455]$ (Table~\ref{tab:ic-medianes}). The
\emph{location} of the peak is less certain: the interval at $\Delta e = 0.141$,
$[231.5 ; 307]$, overlaps the one at $\Delta e = 0.085$ over $[281 ; 307]$, so the two
cells are not separated by the rule used here and the claim is that a peak exists at low
amplitude, not that it sits at one particular cell. That negative statement is weak, and
the control quantifies how weak: at a ratio of medians of $1.32$, which is exactly
$346/263$, the rule separates two samples of $100$ in only $23.4\%$ of $500$ repetitions
on a log-normal law. Failing to separate these two cells is therefore close to
uninformative, whereas the separation of $\Delta e = 0.028$ from $\Delta e = 0.085$ is
not: the rule falsely separates two samples of the same law in $1.0\%$ of repetitions. The relationship is therefore worse
than merely curved, which would already understate Pearson's $r$. No single linear
coefficient summarises it, and its sign depends on which part of the grid is included.
This matches what is established elsewhere about the power-law fit of the article, whose
measured-to-predicted ratio is $0.19$ at the lowest amplitude and $2.08$ at
$\Delta e = 0.141$: the fit holds only mid-grid. Figure~\ref{fig:boxplot} shows the full
distribution at every amplitude.

\paragraph{Extreme values.} At low amplitude the durations are large and widely dispersed.
Pearson's coefficient is a ratio of moments and is not robust, so a handful of runs in the
tail move it substantially. Both rank coefficients are unaffected.

\paragraph{Administrative censoring at $H$.} A duration never reached within the horizon
has no numerical value. $\tau_{\mathrm{swap}}(75\%)$ is censored in $9.10\%$ of runs
overall and in up to $78\%$ at a single amplitude; $\tau_{\mathrm{det}}$ at $\lambda = 50$
is censored in $99.95\%$.

\paragraph{Which survives stratification.} The subject invites the reader to check what
remains once $\Delta e$ is held constant within a stratum. The first feature does not
survive: the non-monotonicity is non-monotonicity \emph{in $\Delta e$}, and within a
stratum $\Delta e$ is fixed by construction. The other two survive, and they are objections
of different kinds. Extreme values make Pearson's coefficient inefficient: it can still be
computed, it is merely unstable and moves with a few runs. Censoring makes it undefined,
because a censored run has no numerical value to enter the sum of products, so the
coefficient cannot be formed without first inventing one. The second objection carries the
argument, since the first is a matter of degree and the second a matter of definition. It
is also the objection that bites on our data, where $\tau_{\mathrm{swap}}(75\%)$ is
censored in nearly four runs out of five at $\Delta e = 0.085$.

Both rank coefficients handle censoring the same way and for the same reason. The horizon
$H$ is identical for every run, so a censored duration genuinely exceeds every observed
one and censored runs may be placed at the last rank, tied among themselves. The argument
would collapse under a variable horizon, where a censored value is only known to exceed
\emph{its own} horizon and no ranking follows. That is where the choice between Spearman
and Kendall becomes decisive.

\section{Definition of $\tau_b$}

For $n$ pairs, let $C$ be the number of concordant pairs, $D$ the discordant ones, and
$n_0 = n(n-1)/2$. Let $t_x$ count the pairs tied on $x$ only and $t_y$ those tied on $y$
only, pairs tied on both sides being counted in each. Then
\begin{equation}
  \tau_b = \frac{C - D}{\sqrt{(n_0 - t_x)(n_0 - t_y)}}.
  \label{eq:taub}
\end{equation}
The denominator distinguishes $\tau_b$ from the ordinary $\tau_a$: each tie block is
removed from the count on the side where it occurs, so the coefficient is normalised by
the pairs that could in principle have been ordered on both sides. Spearman's $\rho$ has no
such correction. It is Pearson's coefficient computed on mid-ranks, and a tie block simply
receives a common mid-rank that then enters a sum of squares as an ordinary value.

\section{Two Tie Structures, Treated Separately}

The comparators of Q~D do not present the same tie structure, and the consequence differs.

\paragraph{Ties on both sides: $\tau_{\mathrm{ARF}}$ against $\tau_{\mathrm{swap}}(q)$.}
Both are durations and both can be censored at $H$. Censored runs form a tie block on the
$y$ side. $\tau_{\mathrm{ARF}}$ is never censored ($0.0000$), but it is discrete and takes
only $321$ distinct values over $2\,000$ runs, so it carries tie blocks of its own.
Equation~\eqref{eq:taub} corrects on both sides and the compensation is complete: the
coefficient is normalised by exactly the set of orderable pairs.

\paragraph{Ties on one side: $\tau_{\mathrm{ARF}}$ against $A(H)$ and $S_{\max}(H)$.}
These two are defined on every run and never censored: $2\,000$ values out of $2\,000$,
with $1\,389$ and $1\,544$ distinct values respectively. There is no tie block on their
side at all, so the correction applies to the $x$ side only. The denominator still counts
the orderable pairs, but the two sides are no longer symmetric: ties in
$\tau_{\mathrm{ARF}}$ remove pairs from the numerator without any counterpart, and the
coefficient is displaced \emph{towards zero} relative to a tie-free measurement of the same
association. The direction matters for reading Q~D.3, where the coefficients reported for
$A(H)$ and $S_{\max}(H)$ are conservative: a value indistinguishable from zero there cannot
be an artefact of ties inflating it.

\section{The Counter-Example, and an Error in the Prescribed Exercise}
\label{sec:contre}

Ten fictitious runs on a common horizon $H = 2\,000$: four observed, six censored. The
exercise as prescribed asks for the four observed values to be perfectly ordered. Carried
out literally, with the censored runs tied on both sides, it yields
$\rho = \tau_b = 1.000000$ exactly. The gap is zero and the counter-example demonstrates
nothing.

The reason is sharper than ``the example is badly chosen''. Ties on both sides require
$\tau_{\mathrm{ARF}}$ itself to be censored, and on this campaign it never is:
$\texttt{censored\_arf} = 0.0000$ on all $2\,000$ runs. The configuration under which the
prescribed exercise produces no gap cannot occur in the data it is meant to illustrate.

Two things separate the three rows, and they should not be conflated. What removes the gap
entirely is the tie structure: as soon as $\tau_{\mathrm{ARF}}$ is left uncensored, which
is the situation that actually holds, the two coefficients part company by $0.071$ even
with the observed runs perfectly ordered. What then amplifies the gap is the ordering of
the observed runs: with four anti-ordered observations, Spearman reports $0.751$ where
$\tau_b$ reports $0.490$, a gap of $0.261$, nearly four times the ordered case.

The mechanism is the same in both rows. Spearman overstates the association because the
six censored runs share a mid-rank that enters its sums of squares as an ordinary value,
manufacturing agreement out of a tie block, while $\tau_b$ removes those pairs from both
counts. The prescribed exercise hides this by silently assuming ties on both sides, which
cancels the effect exactly.

\section{Conclusion}

Pearson is excluded by definition rather than by degree, since censored runs carry no
numerical value. Spearman is computable but overstates the association in the presence of
tie blocks, and the tie blocks here are created by the censoring itself. Kendall's $\tau_b$
is used throughout Q~D, with censored runs imputed to the last rank, which is legitimate
because the horizon is common to all runs and only for that reason.
