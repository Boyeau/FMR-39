\section{Why Pearson Is Excluded}

Three features of the data bear on the choice, and they do not survive stratification
equally.

\emph{Non-monotonicity.} The median $\tau_{\mathrm{ARF}}$ is $118$ steps at
$\Delta e = 0.028$, rises to $346$ at $\Delta e = 0.085$, then falls to $28$ at the top of
the grid: range $28$ to $346$, a factor of $12.4$, not monotone. The rise is not an
artefact of the median (means $162.7$ then $385.9$; bootstrap intervals on the two medians
disjoint, $[93.5 ; 158]$ against $[281 ; 451]$, $10\,000$ resamples). The \emph{location}
of the peak is less certain, since $346$ at $\Delta e = 0.085$ and $263$ at
$\Delta e = 0.141$ are not separable: a peak exists at low amplitude, but not demonstrably
at one cell. No single linear coefficient summarises such a relation, and its sign depends
on which part of the grid is included.

\emph{Extreme values.} At low amplitude the durations are large and widely dispersed, and
Pearson's coefficient, a ratio of moments, moves with a handful of tail runs. Rank
coefficients do not.

\emph{Censoring at $H$.} A duration never reached has no numerical value.
$\tau_{\mathrm{swap}}(75\%)$ is censored in $9.10\%$ of runs overall and up to $78\%$ at a
single amplitude; $\tau_{\mathrm{det}}$ at $\lambda = 50$ in $99.95\%$.

Within a stratum $\Delta e$ is fixed, so the first feature disappears: the non-monotonicity
is non-monotonicity \emph{in $\Delta e$}. The other two survive, and they differ in kind.
Extreme values make Pearson inefficient, since it can still be computed and is merely
unstable. Censoring makes it undefined, since a censored run has no value to enter the sum
of products and the coefficient cannot be formed without inventing one. The second carries
the argument, and it is the one that bites here, where $\tau_{\mathrm{swap}}(75\%)$ is
censored in nearly four runs out of five at $\Delta e = 0.085$.

Both rank coefficients handle censoring the same way: the horizon $H$ is identical for
every run, so a censored duration genuinely exceeds every observed one and censored runs
take the last rank, tied among themselves. Q~D.4 states what this argument rests on and
when it would fail.

\section{Definition, and the Two Tie Structures}

For $n$ pairs, with $C$ concordant, $D$ discordant, $n_0 = n(n-1)/2$, $t_x$ the pairs tied
on $x$ only and $t_y$ those tied on $y$ only:
\begin{equation}
  \tau_b = \frac{C - D}{\sqrt{(n_0 - t_x)(n_0 - t_y)}}.
  \label{eq:taub}
\end{equation}
Each tie block is removed from the count on the side where it occurs, so the coefficient is
normalised by the pairs that could have been ordered on both sides. Spearman's $\rho$ has
no such correction: it is Pearson computed on mid-ranks, and a tie block receives a common
mid-rank that then enters a sum of squares as an ordinary value.

The comparators of Q~D split into two cases. Against $\tau_{\mathrm{swap}}(q)$, both sides
can be censored at $H$; $\tau_{\mathrm{ARF}}$ is never censored ($0.0000$) but is discrete,
taking $321$ distinct values over $2\,000$ runs, so both sides carry tie blocks and
\eqref{eq:taub} corrects on both. Against $A(H)$ and $S_{\max}(H)$, which are defined on
every run and never censored ($1\,389$ and $1\,544$ distinct values), there is no tie block
on their side at all: the correction applies to the $x$ side only, ties in
$\tau_{\mathrm{ARF}}$ remove pairs from the numerator without counterpart, and the
coefficient is displaced \emph{towards zero}. That direction matters for reading Q~D.3,
where those coefficients are therefore conservative: a value indistinguishable from zero
cannot be an artefact of ties inflating it.

\section{Counter-Example, and an Error in the Prescribed Exercise}

Ten fictitious runs on a common horizon, four observed and six censored. Read literally,
with the censored runs tied on both sides, the prescribed exercise yields
$\rho = \tau_b = 1.000000$: no gap, no demonstration. The reason is sharper than a badly
chosen example. Ties on both sides require $\tau_{\mathrm{ARF}}$ itself to be censored, and
on this campaign it never is ($\texttt{censored\_arf} = 0.0000$ on $2\,000$ runs). The
configuration under which the exercise shows nothing cannot occur in the data it
illustrates.

\begin{table}[htbp]
  \centering
  \begin{tabular}{lccc}
    \toprule
    Configuration & $\rho$ (Spearman) & $\tau_b$ (Kendall) & gap \\
    \midrule
    Prescribed, read literally (ties both sides) & $1.000000$ & $1.000000$ & $0.000000$ \\
    Observed ordered, $\tau_{\mathrm{ARF}}$ uncensored & $0.887625$ & $0.816497$ & $0.071129$ \\
    Observed anti-ordered & $0.751068$ & $0.489898$ & $0.261170$ \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
  \caption{Definition and \texttt{scipy} agree to $2.2 \times 10^{-16}$ or better in every
  cell, which is the control on the calculation.}
  \label{tab:contre}
\end{table}

Two things separate the rows, and conflating them is easy. What removes the gap entirely is
the tie structure: once $\tau_{\mathrm{ARF}}$ is left uncensored, which is the situation
that holds, the coefficients part company by $0.071$ even with the observed runs perfectly
ordered. What amplifies the gap is the ordering: anti-ordered, Spearman reports $0.751$
against $0.490$, a gap of $0.261$, nearly four times the ordered case. The mechanism is the
same in both, since the six censored runs share a mid-rank that enters Spearman's sums of
squares as an ordinary value and manufactures agreement out of a tie block, while $\tau_b$
removes those pairs from both counts. The prescribed exercise hides this by assuming ties
on both sides, which cancels the effect exactly.

Pearson is therefore excluded by definition, Spearman overstates the association in the
presence of tie blocks created by the censoring itself, and $\tau_b$ is used throughout
Q~D.
