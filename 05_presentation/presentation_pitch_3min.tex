\begin{frame}{Concept drift: the rule changes, the data does not}
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[height=0.60\textheight]{Fig_slide_drift.png}
  \end{center}
  \begin{center}
    A model trained on the left keeps predicting, confidently, and is now
    \alert{wrong on the shaded band}.
  \end{center}
  \note{First, the phenomenon. A model learns a rule: this input goes with
    that answer. Then the world changes and the rule no longer holds. That
    is concept drift, and here is the cleanest version of it, the one we
    simulate. Two inputs, a straight boundary, a label on each side. The
    change moves the boundary, and nothing else. Only the shaded band has
    swapped sides, and a model trained before is now wrong on it.}
\end{frame}

% ---------------------------------------------------------------- 33 s
\begin{frame}{Defence one: a model that repairs itself}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item A \alert{decision tree} splits the picture with simple cuts, and
          answers by asking which piece a new point falls into.
    \item One tree is fragile, so ten of them \alert{vote}. That is the
          Adaptive Random Forest, the model we study.
    \item \alert{It repairs itself}: a tree whose answers start going wrong
          is thrown away, and a fresh one grows in its place.
  \end{itemize}
  \vfill
  \begin{center}
    \large Ten trees, replaced one at a time. Remember that number.
  \end{center}
  \note{The first defence repairs itself. Its building block is a decision
    tree: it splits the picture with simple cuts, and answers by asking
    which piece a new point falls into. One tree is fragile, so ten of them
    vote. That is the Adaptive Random Forest, the model we study. A tree
    whose answers start going wrong is thrown away, and a fresh one grows in
    its place. Ten trees, replaced one at a time.}
\end{frame}

% ---------------------------------------------------------------- 25 s
\begin{frame}{Defence two: a watchdog that raises the alarm}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item It reads the model's mistakes, one observation at a time, and
          \alert{adds up the excess}.
    \item Once the pile crosses a \alert{threshold}, it calls a human: audit,
          retraining, pause.
  \end{itemize}
  \vfill
  \begin{center}
    \large The alarm is the only thing a human ever sees.\\[0.3em]
    \normalsize Two communities, two tools, never measured against each other.
  \end{center}
  \note{The second defence comes from a different community. A watchdog
    reads the model's mistakes, one observation at a time, and adds up the
    excess. Once that pile crosses a threshold it calls a human: audit,
    retraining, pause. That alarm is the only thing a human ever sees. Two
    communities, two tools, never measured against each other.}
\end{frame}

% ---------------------------------------------------------------- 35 s
\begin{frame}{Put them together, and they cancel out}
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[height=0.70\textheight]{Fig_slide_mecanisme.png}
  \end{center}
  \vspace{-0.4em}
  \begin{center}
    \small schematic
  \end{center}
  \note{Put the two together, and they cancel out. The boundary moves, the
    mistakes begin: top curve. The watchdog needs exactly those mistakes.
    But the forest repairs itself, the mistakes stop, and the pile stops
    growing before it is high enough. The repair erased the evidence. The
    better the model fixes itself, the more surely the alarm stays silent.}
\end{frame}

% ---------------------------------------------------------------- 34 s
\begin{frame}{Why not just lower the threshold?}
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=0.80\textwidth]{Fig_slide_seuils.png}
  \end{center}
  \vspace{-0.3em}
  \begin{center}
    A low threshold also fires when \alert{nothing has happened}, and an
    alarm that cries wolf gets switched off.
  \end{center}
  \note{Does the alarm ever fire? That depends on the threshold. We re-ran
    the benchmark at three settings, two thousand runs each. At the highest
    it fired three times out of two thousand. Lower it, and it fires almost
    every time. So why not lower it? Because a low threshold also fires when
    nothing happened, and an alarm that cries wolf gets switched off. The
    setting quiet enough to be trusted is the one that never sees the
    drift.}
\end{frame}

% ---------------------------------------------------------------- 33 s
\begin{frame}{Our contribution: the accepted clock is wrong}
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{Fig_slide_deux_horloges.png}
  \end{center}
  \vspace{0.2em}
  \begin{center}
    \alert{\large It marks the first tree out of ten, not a repaired forest.}
  \end{center}
  \note{To study a race you need a stopwatch, and that is where we found
    something. The date the literature uses to say the model has recovered
    is the moment the very first tree is replaced: one out of ten. Wait
    until three quarters of the forest has been renewed, and you wait
    roughly ten times longer. That stopwatch does not time a repair. It
    times the start of one.}
\end{frame}

% ---------------------------------------------------------------- 22 s
\begin{frame}{Where we stand}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item The paradox is \alert{real and reproducible}, and we can say when
          the evidence falls short of what the alarm needs.
    \item The clock the field relies on is \alert{biased, always the same
          way}. Every conclusion drawn from it inherits that bias.
    \item \textbf{Next}: watch the model and the watchdog on one common
          clock, and turn this into advice a practitioner can use.
  \end{itemize}
  \vfill
  \begin{center}
    \large Thank you.
  \end{center}

\end{frame}
