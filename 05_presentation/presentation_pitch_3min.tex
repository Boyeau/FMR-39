% ---------------------------------------------------------------- 32 s
\begin{frame}{Concept drift: the rule changes, the data does not}
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[height=0.60\textheight]{Fig_slide_drift.png}
  \end{center}
  \begin{center}
    A model trained on the left keeps predicting, confidently, and is now
    \alert{wrong on the shaded band}.
  \end{center}
  \note{First, the phenomenon. A model learns a rule: this input goes with
    that answer. Then the world changes and the rule no longer holds. That
    is concept drift. Here is the cleanest version of it, and it is what we
    simulate: two inputs, a straight boundary, a label on each side. The
    change moves the boundary, and nothing else. Only the shaded band has
    swapped sides, and a model trained before is now confidently wrong on
    it.}
\end{frame}

% ---------------------------------------------------------------- 33 s
\begin{frame}{Two defences, built by two separate communities}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item \alert{A model that repairs itself.} A forest of ten small
          decision trees. When a tree starts failing, it is thrown away and
          a fresh one takes its place.
    \item \alert{A watchdog that raises the alarm.} It reads the model's
          mistakes, adds up the excess, and calls for a human once the pile
          crosses a threshold.
  \end{itemize}
  \vfill
  \begin{center}
    \large The alarm is the only thing a human ever sees.
  \end{center}
  \note{There are two defences against drift, built by two separate
    communities. First, a model that repairs itself: a forest of ten small
    decision trees, and a tree that starts failing is thrown away and
    replaced. Second, a watchdog: it reads the model's mistakes, adds up the
    excess, and calls for a human once the pile crosses a threshold. That
    alarm is the only thing a human ever sees.}
\end{frame}

% ---------------------------------------------------------------- 35 s
\begin{frame}{Put them together, and they cancel out}
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[height=0.70\textheight]{Fig_slide_mecanisme.png}
  \end{center}
  \vspace{-0.4em}
  \begin{center}
    \small schematic
  \end{center}
  \note{Put the two together, and they cancel out. The boundary moves, the
    mistakes begin: that is the top curve. The watchdog needs exactly those
    mistakes. But the forest repairs itself, the mistakes stop, and the pile
    stops growing before it is high enough. The repair erased the evidence
    the watchdog was waiting for. The better the model fixes itself, the
    more surely the alarm stays silent.}
\end{frame}

% ---------------------------------------------------------------- 30 s
\begin{frame}{How often? We ran the experiment 2\,000 times}
  \begin{center}
    {\small we move the boundary at an instant we choose, then watch whether
     the alarm ever fires}

    \vspace{0.6em}
    {\fontsize{38}{46}\selectfont it fired \alert{\textbf{3}} times\\[0.1em]
     out of \alert{\textbf{2\,000}} runs}
  \end{center}
  \vfill
  \begin{itemize}
    \item One community measures how fast a model recovers. The other
          measures how fast an alarm fires.
    \item \alert{Neither had measured them against each other.}
  \end{itemize}
  \note{How often? We re-ran the benchmark of the paper we are studying.
    Two thousand runs, and in each one we move the boundary at an instant we
    choose, so we know exactly when the change happened. The alarm fired
    three times out of two thousand. This is the normal case. Nobody had
    caught it because one community measures how fast a model recovers, the
    other how fast an alarm fires, and neither measured them together.}
\end{frame}

% ---------------------------------------------------------------- 33 s
\begin{frame}{Our contribution: the accepted clock is wrong}
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{Fig_slide_deux_horloges.png}
  \end{center}
  \vspace{0.2em}
  \begin{center}
    \alert{\large It marks the first tree out of ten, not a repaired forest.}
  \end{center}
  \note{To study a race you need a stopwatch, and that is where we found
    something. The forest replaces its trees one at a time. The date the
    literature uses to say the model has recovered is the moment the very
    first tree is replaced: one out of ten. Wait until three quarters of the
    forest has been renewed, and you wait roughly ten times longer. That
    stopwatch does not time a repair. It times the start of one.}
\end{frame}

% ---------------------------------------------------------------- 22 s
\begin{frame}{Where we stand}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item The paradox is \alert{real and reproducible}, and we can say when
          the evidence falls short of what the alarm needs.
    \item The clock the field relies on is \alert{biased, always the same
          way}. Every conclusion drawn from it inherits that bias.
    \item \textbf{Next}: watch the model and the watchdog on one common
          clock, and turn this into advice a practitioner can use.
  \end{itemize}
  \vfill
  \begin{center}
    \large Thank you.
  \end{center}
  \note{Where we stand. The paradox is real and reproducible, and we can
    say when the evidence falls short. The clock the field relies on is
    biased, always the same way, so every conclusion drawn from it inherits
    that bias. What remains is to watch both on one common clock, and turn
    this into advice a practitioner can use. Thank you.}
\end{frame}
