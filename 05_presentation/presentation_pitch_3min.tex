\begin{frame}{Concept drift: the rule changes, the data does not}
  \vspace{-0.6em}
  \[
    \underbrace{P(X)}_{\text{the data}}\ \text{unchanged}
    \qquad\qquad
    \underbrace{P(Y \mid X)}_{\text{the rule}}\ \text{changes}
  \]
  \vspace{-0.9em}
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[height=0.46\textheight]{Fig_slide_drift.png}
  \end{center}
  \begin{center}
    A model trained on the left keeps predicting, confidently, and is now
    \alert{wrong on the shaded band}.
  \end{center}
  \note{First, the problem. A model learns a rule from data. Then the
    world changes, and the rule becomes wrong: that is concept drift. In
    symbols, the inputs keep the same distribution, P of X, but the
    rule, P of Y given X, changes. Here is the simple case we simulate.
    Points are split by a line. At the change, the line moves. The
    points in the shaded band switch labels, so a model trained before
    the change now gets them wrong.}
\end{frame}

% ---------------------------------------------------------------- 33 s
\begin{frame}{Defence one: a model that repairs itself}
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=0.94\textwidth]{Fig_slide_foret.png}
  \end{center}
  \vspace{-0.2em}
  \begin{center}
    This is the \alert{Adaptive Random Forest}. Ten trees, replaced one at a
    time.
  \end{center}
  \note{The first defence is a model that repairs itself: the Adaptive
    Random Forest. It is made of ten decision trees that vote. A single
    tree can only draw step-shaped borders, so it is never perfect. When
    one tree starts making too many mistakes, it is thrown away and a
    new one grows in its place.}
\end{frame}

% ---------------------------------------------------------------- 25 s
\begin{frame}{Defence two: a watchdog that raises the alarm}
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=0.94\textwidth]{Fig_slide_watchdog.png}
  \end{center}
  \vspace{-0.2em}
  \begin{center}
    Two communities, two tools. \alert{What happens when both run at once?}
  \end{center}
  \note{The second defence is a watchdog. Each mistake adds to a pile,
    each correct answer removes a little, never below zero. If the pile
    crosses a threshold, the alarm goes off and a human steps in. That
    alarm is all the human ever sees. So what happens when both defences
    run together?}
\end{frame}

% ---------------------------------------------------------------- 35 s
\begin{frame}{Put them together, and they cancel out}
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[height=0.70\textheight]{Fig_slide_mecanisme.png}
  \end{center}
  \vspace{-0.4em}
  \begin{center}
    \alert{The repair has a date. The alarm never gets one.}
    {\small\hspace{1em}(schematic)}
  \end{center}
  \note{Here is what happens. The line moves, the mistakes start, and the
    pile begins to grow. But the forest replaces its trees, the mistakes
    stop, and the pile drains back to zero before it reaches the
    threshold. It is a race between the repair and the alarm. With a
    strict threshold, the repair almost always wins, and nobody is
    warned.}
\end{frame}

% ---------------------------------------------------------------- 34 s
\begin{frame}{Why not just lower the threshold?}
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=0.68\textwidth]{Fig_slide_seuils.png}
  \end{center}
  \vspace{-0.3em}
  \begin{center}
    A low threshold also fires when \alert{nothing has happened}, and an
    alarm that cries wolf gets switched off.
  \end{center}
  \note{So why not simply lower the threshold? We re-ran the paper's
    experiment at three thresholds, two thousand runs each. With the
    strictest one, the alarm went off three times in two thousand. With
    a lower one, it goes off almost every time. But a low threshold also
    goes off when nothing has changed, and an alarm that cries wolf ends
    up ignored. The paper concludes that no threshold is both
    trustworthy and sensitive.}
\end{frame}

% ---------------------------------------------------------------- 33 s
\begin{frame}{Our contribution: when is the forest actually repaired?}
  \begin{center}
    \small To call the race, you have to \alert{time the repair}.
  \end{center}
  \vspace{-0.1em}
  \begin{center}
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{Fig_slide_deux_horloges.png}
  \end{center}
  \begin{center}
    \alert{\large It fires at the \emph{first} tree. It times the start of a
    repair, not its end.}
  \end{center}
  \note{To say who wins the race, you have to time the repair. The paper
    times it at the moment the first of the ten trees is replaced. But
    one tree out of ten is only the start of the repair. Wait until
    three quarters of the forest is renewed, and you wait fourteen times
    longer. The paper's stopwatch stops too early, and always in the
    forest's favour.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Measuring the repair in mistakes, not in trees}
  \begin{center}
    \small
    \begin{tabular}{@{}llr@{}}
      \toprule
      Measure & What it counts & Value \\
      \midrule
      \textbf{First tree replaced} {\scriptsize\color{gray}$\tau_{\mathrm{ARF}}$}
        & the date the paper uses & $86$ \\
      \textbf{Error back to normal} {\scriptsize\color{gray}$\tau_{\mathrm{rec}}$}
        & the error settles back to its usual level & $261$ \\
      \textbf{Forest 3/4 renewed} {\scriptsize\color{gray}$\tau_{\mathrm{swap}}$}
        & 8 trees out of 10 replaced & $1\,225$ \\
      \midrule
      \textbf{Excess mistakes} {\scriptsize\color{gray}$A(H)$}
        & the extra errors the change caused & $38$ \\
      \textbf{Watchdog's peak} {\scriptsize\color{gray}$S_{\max}(H)$}
        & the highest point of the watchdog's pile & $33$ \\
      \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}

    \vspace{0.2em}
    {\scriptsize\color{gray} Same change as the previous slide, median of 100 runs.
    First three: observations after the change. Excess mistakes: counted over the
    next $2\,000$ observations. Watchdog thresholds: 8, 25 and 50.}
  \end{center}
  \begin{center}
    \alert{The paper's date says nothing about the damage.}
  \end{center}
  \note{So we also measure the repair in mistakes. Same change as before.
    The first tree goes after 86 observations. The error is back to
    normal only after 261, and three quarters of the forest after 1,225.
    The change costs about 38 extra mistakes. And the paper's stopwatch
    says nothing about that cost, at any of the eighteen change sizes we
    tested.}
\end{frame}
